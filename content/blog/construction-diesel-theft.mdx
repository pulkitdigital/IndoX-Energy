Diesel is one of the few site supplies that is valuable, easy to move and hard to trace once it leaves the tank. On a busy construction project it passes through many hands: the supplier, the storekeeper, the operator, the night guard. Every handover is a place where a few litres can disappear without anyone noticing.

Most sites do not have a theft problem so much as a visibility problem. Fuel is bought, stored and issued, but the records that connect those steps are thin. This article walks through where diesel usually goes missing, how to spot it early, and the controls that make losses visible.

## Where diesel goes missing on site

Losses rarely come from one dramatic event. They build up quietly at points where nobody checks the numbers.

### At delivery

If nobody on site confirms the quantity received, short deliveries go unnoticed. A tanker that unloads less than the invoice shows is the simplest form of loss, and the hardest to prove after the fact.

### In storage

Open or unlocked tanks, drums kept in the open and tanks without a reliable level reading all invite small, regular draws. Leaks and spills also count as losses, even when nobody took the fuel on purpose.

### At the point of issue

When fuel is issued without recording which machine received it, a generator or excavator can be "filled" twice on paper, or the difference between what was drawn and what reached the machine can walk away in a jerrycan.

### From the machine itself

Fuel can be drained from equipment tanks at night or during idle periods. Without any link between fuel issued and hours worked, this is almost invisible.

## Warning signs to watch for

You do not need special equipment to notice that something is off. Look for patterns such as:

- Fuel consumption that rises while the work done stays the same.
- Machines that need refilling far more often than similar machines on the same site.
- Tank levels that drop overnight or over weekends when nothing is running.
- Delivery quantities that never quite match what the tank level shows afterwards.
- Registers filled in at the end of the day, in one handwriting, with round numbers.

None of these proves theft on its own. They tell you where to look.

<Callout type="tip" title="Start with one comparison">
Put the fuel issued to each major machine next to the hours it worked over the same week. Machines that stand out from the rest are the ones to check first.
</Callout>

## Controls that make losses visible

Good controls do two things: they make taking fuel harder, and they make it easier to see when it happens anyway. You can build them up in layers.

| Control | What it stops | What it needs |
|---|---|---|
| Witnessed, metered deliveries | Short deliveries | A person on site to check the meter and sign |
| Locked, secured storage | Casual draws from tanks and drums | Lockable tank fittings and key control |
| Issue register per machine | "Double" fills and unrecorded issues | A register kept at the point of issue |
| Automatic tank level readings | Unnoticed drops in stock | A level sensor or automatic tank gauge |
| Tagged dispensing | Fills to unauthorized machines | A dispensing unit with tag control, where set up |
| Regular reconciliation | Losses hiding in the paperwork | Someone who compares receipts, issues and stock |

### Record every movement

The foundation is simple: every litre that arrives, every litre that is issued and the stock left at the end of the day, written down at the time it happens. Opening stock plus receipts minus issues should equal closing stock. When it does not, you have a number to investigate.

### Measure instead of estimate

Dipsticks and eyeballing a gauge are better than nothing, but they are slow and easy to fudge. An automatic tank gauge reads the level continuously, so an unusual drop shows up the same day rather than at month-end.

### Tie fuel to machines

When each fill is recorded against a specific machine, consumption per machine becomes visible. Over a few weeks you learn what normal looks like for each one, and outliers stand out.

## Building a routine that sticks

Controls only work if people follow them on a Tuesday night as well as on the day of an audit. A few habits help:

- [ ] Name one person responsible for fuel records at each site.
- [ ] Check deliveries against the meter every time, not only when something seems wrong.
- [ ] Reconcile stock daily on active sites and weekly on quieter ones.
- [ ] Review consumption per machine at a set point each week.
- [ ] Follow up on every unexplained gap, even small ones, and write down what you found.

Share the results with site supervisors. When people know the numbers are checked, casual losses tend to fall on their own.

## When to bring in technology

Manual records can take a small site a long way. As the number of machines, tanks and shifts grows, paper struggles to keep up. That is usually the point to consider automatic level monitoring, tagged dispensing and a dashboard that flags unusual movement, depending on your site conditions and budget.

Technology does not replace the routine above; it makes the routine faster and harder to skip. Alerts arrive while there is still a trail to follow, and reconciliation takes minutes instead of an afternoon.

## Closing thoughts

Diesel losses on construction sites are common because fuel moves through many hands with few checks. Start by recording every movement, measure stock instead of guessing, and link fuel to the machines that use it. Once the numbers are visible, most losses become much easier to prevent.
