Walk into any purchase discussion about industrial fuel and three abbreviations come up again and again: HSD, LDO and MHO. They are often mentioned in the same breath, and they can look similar in a drum. But they are made for different jobs, and putting the wrong one into a machine or burner can cause poor performance, extra maintenance or worse.

This guide explains, in plain terms, what each fuel is generally used for and how to approach choosing between them. It does not replace your equipment manufacturer's guidance, which should always be the final word.

<Callout type="warning" title="Check the manufacturer's guidance first">
Always confirm the fuel type against the equipment manufacturer's manual or specification sheet before ordering. Fuel requirements differ by equipment, and using a fuel the maker does not approve can affect performance, maintenance and warranty.
</Callout>

## What each fuel is, in plain terms

### HSD: high-speed diesel

HSD is the diesel most people mean when they say "diesel". It is made for high-speed diesel engines: trucks, cars, excavators, loaders, cranes, pumps and most diesel generator (DG) sets. If a machine has a modern diesel engine, HSD is usually what it runs on.

### LDO: light diesel oil

LDO is a heavier distillate than HSD. It is generally used in slower-speed engines and in industrial heating equipment, such as some furnaces and boilers. It is not a like-for-like substitute for HSD in modern high-speed engines.

### MHO: mixed hydrocarbon oil

MHO is an industrial fuel generally used for heating applications, such as furnaces, boilers and similar burners in manufacturing. It is chosen where the equipment has been designed or set up to burn it.

<PullQuote>The right fuel is the one your equipment was built to burn, not the one that happens to be cheapest this month.</PullQuote>

## How to choose the right fuel

Choosing a fuel is less about comparing the fuels and more about understanding your equipment.

### Start with the equipment

List every machine, generator, furnace and boiler that uses fuel on your site. For each one, find the manufacturer's recommended fuel in the manual, on the nameplate or from the supplier.

### Separate engines from heating equipment

Engines, especially high-speed ones, are usually specified for HSD. Heating equipment such as furnaces and boilers is where LDO and MHO are more often considered, depending on how the burner is designed.

### Think about storage and handling

If a site uses more than one fuel, each needs its own clearly labelled storage and dispensing, to prevent mix-ups. Plan for this before ordering a second grade.

### Consider supply and availability

Not every grade is available everywhere or in every quantity. Availability depends on location and applicable regulations, so confirm with your supplier early.

### Keep records by fuel type

Track receipts and consumption separately for each fuel. This keeps costs clear and makes it easier to spot problems in any one system.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Assuming one "diesel" suits every machine on the site.
- Switching fuel to save money without checking the equipment specification.
- Storing different fuels in unlabelled or shared tanks.
- Mixing fuels in the same tank or drum.
- Losing track of which delivery went into which tank.

## Closing thoughts

HSD, LDO and MHO each have their place. HSD powers most engines on site, while LDO and MHO are generally found in industrial heating and specific engines designed for them. The safest way to choose is to start from what your equipment maker specifies, then plan storage, supply and records around it.
